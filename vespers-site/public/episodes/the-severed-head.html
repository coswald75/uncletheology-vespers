Transcript

Welcome to Vespers, where we take up the last hour of your day.

Tonight: headship — the head and the body, what a head owes, and what happens when the head is severed.

A reminder: this is a survey of great ideas, not necessarily an endorsement of any one contribution.

Hierarchy is a word our age says through clenched teeth. We suspect it, we apologize for it, we dismantle it wherever we find it — and then we wonder why nobody is looking after anybody. Tonight we take the word out of quarantine and turn it in the light: what the old catechisms meant by superiors, inferiors, and equals; what the apostles meant by the head and the body; what happens in a college, a country, or a marriage when the head abdicates, and what happens when a usurper takes its place. Our map for the evening is a novel — the last of C. S. Lewis's science-fiction trilogy, a book called That Hideous Strength, which is, under all its machinery, a book about exactly this. Lewis is within living memory and his pages are not ours to read at length, so tonight we will walk around his book rather than through it, and let the older voices — the catechism, the prophets, the apostles, the Puritans — do the reading.

But begin with the man, because the man learned about headship the hard way, as a boy.

Lewis grew up in Belfast, in a world that was hierarchy stacked on hierarchy — Anglo and Irish, church and chapel, and over in England that near-sacred line between the men who had gone to the right schools and the men who had not. For a family like his, respectable but not grand, education was the one ladder that could be bought, and a father was the one who chose the ladder. When Lewis was nine years old his mother died. His father, grieving and anxious and not a cruel man, made his decisions about the boys' schooling the way grieving and anxious men often do — quickly, and without going the extra mile of mind that a child's whole formation deserves. Many a child has been wounded not by a father's malice but by a father's unwillingness to think hard on his behalf.

So within weeks of losing his mother, the boy was shipped across the water to a small English boarding school whose headmaster was a clergyman named Capron — a man whose own mind, it later became clear, was failing. The school was arbitrary cruelty administered from a desk. Here is a head in the fullest formal sense — reverend, robed, installed — who had ceased to be a head in any real sense at all. The children were not protected, not provided for, not taught. Lewis carried the marks of that schoolroom for the rest of his life.

Now set beside that ruin the man who saved Lewis's mind. His name was Kirkpatrick — an old headmaster who had once taught Lewis's own father, and who in retirement took boys into his home as a private tutor. He prepared Lewis's older brother for his examinations, and when Lewis's own schooling reached its crisis, the father's easiest good decision was to send the younger boy to the same old man. Lewis arrived expecting a sentimental silver-haired schoolmaster out of a storybook. He got off the train, made an idle remark about the wildness of the scenery — and the old man stopped him and took the remark apart. What did he mean by wildness? On what grounds had he expected anything else? Had he ever seen a map of the county? By the end of the walk the boy understood that he had not been making an observation at all; he had been making noise. Kirkpatrick did nothing but that, for years — question, distinguish, demand grounds — because he was interested in the truth and assumed you were too. The brothers revered him ever after and called him the Great Knock. He was the man who got their minds under control. And mark this, because the whole evening turns on it: he was not nice. He was kind. Those are different things, and the difference is one of the deepest lessons Lewis ever learned. Years later, when he came to write his children's stories, he built the distinction into the very lion at their center: safe, say the talking beasts, is the wrong word for him altogether — but he is good.

A failed head, and a true one. Lewis met both before he was seventeen. Now let us get the grammar straight — the old grammar of order that Lewis inherited and never abandoned.

About two and a half centuries before Lewis was born, an assembly of English and Scottish divines sat down at Westminster and wrote out, in the form of questions and answers, what they understood the Scriptures to teach. When they came to the commandment about honoring father and mother, they did something our age would never dare: they read the whole social order out of it, without apology. Hear the commandment first.

From the book of Exodus, in the King James Version:

Honour thy father and thy mother: that thy days may be long upon the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee.

And now the catechism — the Westminster Larger Catechism, seventeenth century. Asked who is meant by father and mother in that commandment, it answers:

By father and mother, in the fifth commandment, are meant, not only natural parents, but all superiors in age and gifts; and especially such as, by God's ordinance, are over us in place of authority, whether in family, church, or commonwealth.

Asked for the general scope of the commandment, it answers:

The general scope of the fifth commandment is the performance of those duties which we mutually owe in our several relations, as inferiors, superiors, or equals.

There it is, without a blush: inferiors, superiors, equals. The catechism hands us a complete grammar of social existence, and it is hierarchical from top to bottom. Honor and obedience flow upward. Love and provision flow downward. Justice flows across. And notice the phrase our several relations — the duties are owed specifically, not generically. Your superior in one relation may be your inferior in another and your equal in a third; the man who commands the regiment kneels in the pew. It is the opposite of every collectivist scheme in which persons are interchangeable units. Nobody is a unit here. Everybody is a node in a web of particular debts.

And the catechism does not romanticize the upward duty while forgetting the downward one. Hear what inferiors owe:

The honour which inferiors owe to their superiors is, all due reverence in heart, word, and behaviour; prayer and thanksgiving for them; imitation of their virtues and graces; willing obedience to their lawful commands and counsels… bearing with their infirmities, and covering them in love, that so they may be an honour to them and to their government.

Now hear what superiors owe — and ask yourself when you last heard authority described this way:

It is required of superiors, according to that power they receive from God, and that relation wherein they stand, to love, pray for, and bless their inferiors; to instruct, counsel, and admonish them… protecting, and providing for them all things necessary for soul and body: and by grave, wise, holy, and exemplary carriage, to procure glory to God, honour to themselves, and so to preserve that authority which God hath put upon them.

To love, pray for, and bless. To protect and provide, soul and body. That is what the word superior means in this grammar — not a man who gets more, but a man who owes more. And the divines knew exactly how heads go wrong, because they wrote the failure into the catechism too:

The sins of superiors are, besides the neglect of the duties required of them, an inordinate seeking of themselves, their own glory, ease, profit, or pleasure; commanding things unlawful… careless exposing, or leaving them to wrong, temptation, and danger… or any way dishonouring themselves, or lessening their authority, by an unjust, indiscreet, rigorous, or remiss behaviour.

Careless exposing, or leaving them to wrong. Hold that clause; we will need it before the night is out. And for completeness, the duties and sins of equals:

The duties of equals are, to regard the dignity and worth of each other, in giving honour to go one before another; and to rejoice in each other's gifts and advancement, as their own. The sins of equals are… the undervaluing of the worth, envying the gifts, grieving at the advancement or prosperity one of another; and usurping pre-eminence one over another.

Usurping pre-eminence. Hold that clause too. Abdication and usurpation — the catechism has just named the two ways a hierarchy dies, and they are the two halves of the novel we are circling.

But before the novel, the objection. Doesn't all this talk of superiors and inferiors offend against equality? Lewis faced that question squarely in two wartime essays, one called Equality and one called Membership, and his answer has a shape worth carrying in your pocket. He was, he insisted, a democrat — and then he gave the strangest reason ever given for it. In his words: "I am a democrat because I believe in the Fall of Man." Political equality, he argued, is real and necessary — but necessary the way medicine is necessary, not the way food is. We level men politically not because no man is fit to be trusted with unchecked power over his fellows, but because no fallen man is. Equality is a remedy for our disease, not a description of creation. And he pressed the point with a deliberately uncomfortable comparison: the impulse to strip away all hierarchy, he said, is like the impulse behind nudism — it mistakes the covering for the corruption, when in fact the covering is there to protect something fragile and glorious underneath. Strip it away and you do not get honesty; you get exposure.

Underneath this runs a distinction that unlocks the rest of the evening, and the divines and Lewis held it together without strain: equality of worth is not equality of function. Every person is of equal and infinite worth before God. It does not follow that every person is equal in authority, wisdom, capacity, or usefulness to a given task in a given hour. To collapse the two is not kindness; it is a lie — and it is a lie that always ends up serving the strong. For consider the man the old villages called simple. In a frank hierarchy, he is somebody's charge: he is my inferior, says the farmer, of equal worth to God and to me, and therefore mine to look after. In a world that insists he is exactly as capable as every other man, no one owes him anything — he is handed responsibilities he cannot carry and left alone with them, and his life is made crueler by the compliment. Abolish hierarchy, and the people who most need looking after are precisely the ones no longer looked after.

The old commonplace books, as usual, have been quarreling about this for centuries, and it is worth hearing the quarrel — both sides of it.

From Benjamin Franklin: Equal liberty was originally the portion and is still the birthright of all men.

From William Goodell: Christianity recognizes the unity and equality of all men, and seeks to unite them on the basis of that equality and union, under the protection of the common Father of all.

And an old handbook credits Plato with this: All men are by nature equal, all made of the same earth by one Workman; and however we may deceive ourselves, as dear unto God is the poor peasant as the mighty prince.

There is the equality of worth — the peasant as dear as the prince. Now the other column.

From Charles James Fox, the great English champion of liberty: I am for equality; I think men entitled to equal rights, but to unequal things.

From Doctor Johnson: So far is it from being true that men are naturally equal, that no two people can be half an hour together, but one shall acquire an evident superiority over the other.

From Arnold: It is untrue that equality is a law of nature; nature has no equality; its sovereign law is subordination and dependence.

From Bishop: All men are not equal in the faculties of either body or mind; a necessity of superiority and subordination springs from the very nature which God has given us.

From Asgill: If all men were on an equality, the consequence would be that all must perish; for who would till the ground; who would sow it; who would plant; who would press wine?

And Napoleon, who knew something about the subject from the inside: To be equals, with everybody uppermost, is the vanity of all.

Equal in worth, unequal in things — both columns are true, and the whole art of a humane society is refusing to let either one eat the other. Johnson again, in a gentler mood: I am a friend to subordination, as most conducive to the happiness of society; there is a reciprocal pleasure in governing and being governed. And Crabb completes the catechism's thought: it is the part of the exalted to treat the subordinate with indulgence.

Now the novel. That Hideous Strength is set in an English university town in the years after the war, and everything in it — the comedy, the horror, the marriage, the magic — happens inside the question of authority: who is head, what a head owes, and what becomes of a body when its head fails. There are four hierarchies in the book, and once you see them the whole story organizes itself.

The first is the college — Bracton College, a small, old, self-governing fellowship. Its offices are legitimate, its procedures orderly, its meetings punctilious. And it is dead. The men who hold the titles no longer govern; the college is actually run by an inner clique of managers — what we would now call a deep state of bureaucrats — who steer every decision through committee while the nominal heads doze. The great test comes when the college is maneuvered into selling its one treasure, an ancient wood at its heart, and the sale is accomplished not by open argument but by procedural fog: agendas arranged, tired men worn down, the real question never quite put. Remember the catechism's clause — careless exposing, or leaving them to wrong. Bracton is headship on paper and abdication in fact. Nobody seizes the college; the college simply declines to be governed, and so is taken.

The second hierarchy is the thing that takes it: an institute — Lewis gives it an acronym that spells, of all things, the word nice — which presents itself as progress incarnate: national renewal, scientific coordination, humanitarian uplift. In fact it is naked will to power in a lab coat, and Lewis literalizes its nature with an image straight out of a nightmare: the institute's secret sanctuary, the thing its inner circle worships and takes orders from, is a severed human head — a guillotined man's head kept artificially alive on tubes and taps. That is where tonight's title comes from, and you could not ask for a more exact emblem: a command center with no body to answer to and no heart to inform it. Command without communion. All demand and no provision — the downward half of the catechism's duty simply cut away. If Bracton is abdication, this is usurpation: authority seized by those to whom it was never given, over those for whom they feel nothing.

And here Lewis plays a trick worthy of his devil-letters: these people are terrible, and they are pleasant. The institute's men are all warmth and welcome, all first names and shared jokes and flattering confidences. They are nice. They are not kind. Remember the Great Knock, who was kind and not nice; the institute is his exact inversion, and the world is full of both. Some of the proudest, most inwardly dismissive people alive have learned that the language of humility is easy to parrot — the soft voice, the aw-shucks deflection, the studied servant's vocabulary — while the interior remains all appetite. The smiling face above; the machinery below.

Scripture has a portrait of this man, and it is very old. Absalom, the king's son, wanted his father's throne, and this is how he campaigned for it.

From the book of Samuel, in the King James Version:

And it came to pass after this, that Absalom prepared him chariots and horses, and fifty men to run before him. And Absalom rose up early, and stood beside the way of the gate: and it was so, that when any man that had a controversy came to the king for judgment, then Absalom called unto him, and said, Of what city art thou? And he said, Thy servant is of one of the tribes of Israel. And Absalom said unto him, See, thy matters are good and right; but there is no man deputed of the king to hear thee. Absalom said moreover, Oh that I were made judge in the land, that every man which hath any suit or cause might come unto me, and I would do him justice!

He goes on:

And it was so, that when any man came nigh to him to do him obeisance, he put forth his hand, and took him, and kissed him. And on this manner did Absalom to all Israel that came to the king for judgment: so Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel.

Look at the machinery of it. The chariots and the fifty runners — the pomp of a head — and then, when a man bows, the hand on the shoulder, the kiss, the aw-shucks refusal of the very honor he is dressed for: no, brother, none of that; I am just one of you; if only someone would hear your case. It is a total mockery of the realities, flattery running in both directions at once — he flatters the crowd and teaches the crowd to flatter him — and it steals a kingdom. The old moralists knew this figure well.

From Raleigh: Flatterers are the worst kind of traitors, for they will strengthen thy imperfections, encourage thee in all evils, correct thee in nothing.

From William Penn: Avoid flatterers, for they are thieves in disguise; they lie to flatter, and flatter to cheat; and, which is worse, if you believe them, you cheat yourselves most dangerously.

And the proverb sits in judgment over the whole scene.

From the book of Proverbs:

Faithful are the wounds of a friend; but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful.

Kirkpatrick wounded like a friend. Absalom kissed like an enemy. Nice and kind are not the same word.

The third hierarchy in the novel is the smallest and the first one the reader meets: a marriage. A young don named Mark and his wife Jane, six months in and already miles apart. Lewis's diagnosis is ruthlessly evenhanded: this is hierarchy mutually snubbed. Mark has abdicated headship through sheer absence — not cruelty, just a hungry attention that is always somewhere else, scanning for status, for the men who matter, for the room he has not yet been invited into. Whatever love is owed downward and inward, he has quietly rerouted to his career. And Jane, for her part, withholds — honor, receptivity, even plain attention — because she has confused self-sufficiency with dignity; she is determined to owe nothing and to need nothing, and calls the resulting coldness independence. Lewis treats both failures as failures of love, not of etiquette. Neither is oppressing the other. They are starving each other, politely.

And the fourth hierarchy is the answer to the other three: a little household on a hill at the edge of town — the manor of St. Anne's — gathered around a man named Ransom. The name is not subtle, and it is not meant to be; Lewis's hero carries the shape of Christ into the story. He holds authority in that house, real authority — people obey him — and every mark of his authority is the inversion of the institute's. His headship is under authority: he commands because he is himself commanded, obedient to the Power he serves. He bears a wound that will not heal, taken in an earlier battle in an earlier book, and he wears it the way the catechism's superior wears his office — the cost of protecting what was his to protect. And under his roof the whole grammar of the catechism comes alive: every member of the household — a scholar, a doubting skeptic who never does convert, a bear, a charwoman — is fully seen and given real work fitted to what they actually are. The charwoman is not flattered into pretending she is a philosopher; the philosopher is not shamed into pretending he is a charwoman; and no one is interchangeable with anyone. It is the one hierarchy in the book with the downward duty intact — and it is the one place in the book where anybody is happy. That is Lewis's quiet thesis: hierarchy with the love left in is not the enemy of joy but the shape of it.

Now, the severed head. Why is that the image at the center — why a head? Because Lewis is a Christian writing inside the apostle Paul's picture of reality, and in that picture head and body is not a metaphor for management. It is the deep structure of the world. Hear the apostle build it.

From Paul's epistle to the Corinthians, in the King James Version:

For as the body is one, and hath many members, and all the members of that one body, being many, are one body: so also is Christ. For the body is not one member, but many. If the foot shall say, Because I am not the hand, I am not of the body; is it therefore not of the body? And if the ear shall say, Because I am not the eye, I am not of the body; is it therefore not of the body? If the whole body were an eye, where were the hearing? If the whole were hearing, where were the smelling? But now hath God set the members every one of them in the body, as it hath pleased him.

He goes on:

And if they were all one member, where were the body? But now are they many members, yet but one body. And the eye cannot say unto the hand, I have no need of thee: nor again the head to the feet, I have no need of you. Nay, much more those members of the body, which seem to be more feeble, are necessary: that there should be no schism in the body; but that the members should have the same care one for another. And whether one member suffer, all the members suffer with it; or one member be honoured, all the members rejoice with it.

The head cannot say to the feet, I have no need of you. There is the institute's whole creed refuted in a clause — for the severed head in the cellar is precisely a head that has said to every foot on earth, I have no need of you. And notice the members are not equal in function — eye, hand, foot, each set where it pleased God — yet the feeblest are necessary and the same care runs to all. Equal worth, unequal function, mutual need: the catechism's grammar is Paul's grammar.

And Paul tells us where the grammar comes from.

From the epistle to the Ephesians:

But speaking the truth in love, may grow up into him in all things, which is the head, even Christ: from whom the whole body fitly joined together and compacted by that which every joint supplieth, according to the effectual working in the measure of every part, maketh increase of the body unto the edifying of itself in love.

Christ is the head — and from the head the body is nourished, joined, and grown. Headship, in this picture, is not extraction; it is supply. And the same apostle warns what happens when the connection is cut. Of those who drift from Christ he writes:

And not holding the Head, from which all the body by joints and bands having nourishment ministered, and knit together, increaseth with the increase of God.

Not holding the Head. A body that lets go of its head does not become free. It becomes dead. That is the theological engine under the whole novel: severance is not liberation, whichever end does the severing. And Paul dares to carry the pattern into the very room where Mark and Jane are starving each other:

For the husband is the head of the wife, even as Christ is the head of the church: and he is the saviour of the body. Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it.

The head of the wife — even as Christ. Which is to say: the measure of headship is not command but expenditure. He is the saviour of the body; he gave himself for it. Any man who reads that sentence as a license has not read to the end of it. Matthew Henry, commenting on the one-body passage, puts the whole matter in a line: Christ and his church form one body, as head and members; all Christians are dependent one upon another, and the good of the whole should be the object of all.

With that engine in view, watch what the novel actually does with its two abdications — because Lewis has built them in parallel, and the parallel is the loveliest piece of architecture in the book.

Bracton College holds a wood. Bragdon Wood — older than the college, older by legend than Roman Britain, with a well at its heart that the stories tie to Merlin himself. It is hard for a modern reader to feel what earlier ages felt about such a place: a deep honorific reverence for a particular forest, a sense that the college does not so much own the wood as keep it — that the wood is the college's stewardship, the treasure it exists to tend. And the whole plot turns on this: the dark powers gathering in the story do not want the college at all. They flatter the college, they enrich the college, they make its little managers feel important — in order to get the wood. And they do not finally want the wood either. They want what sleeps in it. The college is courted for the sake of the treasure it was set to guard and has forgotten how to value.

Now look at Mark. His wife is the novel's other Bragdon Wood. Jane is something ancient and alive and gifted — she has, without asking for it, a strange endowment: true dreams, visions of things happening far away; Lewis's way, I think, of putting feminine glory into the visible register of magic. She has been entrusted to Mark's care, and his inattention has left her exposed — exposed, as it happens, to the one power in the story that is actively hunting for exactly the gift he has never bothered to notice. For here too the enemy's courtship is misdirected on purpose: the institute recruits Mark, flatters Mark, dangles circles of importance before Mark — and the whole time it is Jane they want. In both cases the pattern is identical: the satanic strategy is congenial, affirming, generous to the negligent steward, feeding him precisely the respect he is hungry for, in order to reach past him to the treasure behind him. The head is bought with honors so the body can be taken.

And in both cases the strategy fails, and it fails the same way — and here is where the God of the story shows his hand. The wood's sleeper wakes, and walks straight past every scheming party to the one true head in the story: he presents himself at St. Anne's and submits to Ransom. And Jane's gift does the very same thing — her dreams themselves orient her toward St. Anne's, lead her, resisting the whole way, to Ransom's door. Neither treasure falls to the hunters. The heads abdicated; Providence did not. The good shepherd of the story goes out and finds what the hired men left exposed — and if you know how Lewis's own life ended, you know he had personal reasons to believe in that pattern. He answered by hand every letter he ever received, thousands upon thousands of them, and a striking number were from women who wrote to a stranger across an ocean about their souls because no one nearer home was tending them. One of those correspondents — a sharp-witted American, converted partly through his books, her own marriage collapsing — he eventually married. Aslan, you might say, keeps seeking out what the failed heads leave in the woods.

One more pass at the enemy, because Lewis distinguishes two grades of the disease, and most of us are more at risk of the second. Call them hard elitism and soft. Hard elitism is the institute's inner circle: men with an explicit, stated creed that they are the vanguard, entitled to remake or simply supersede ordinary humanity by whatever means necessary. That is usurpation as a metaphysic — the claim of a right to rule grounded in nothing above the rulers, not in God, not in love. Every age produces it; ours produces it fluently in the vanguard language of the modern technology world, where the deep-down sentence I know what is better for you than you do comes wrapped, as always, in nice.

But soft elitism is Mark's disease, and it is the common cold of the soul: not a creed but an appetite — the low, constant scanning of every room for who is more inside than he is; the chronic inability to care about what he already has because his attention is bolted to what he has not yet been admitted to. Lewis gave a famous lecture about this appetite in the middle of the war, and his theory of it supplies the mechanics of Mark's whole plot-line: in every institution, he argued, alongside the official hierarchy there is a second, unwritten system — the circle within the circle, the ring within the ring — and the longing to get inside it is insatiable by its nature, because from inside every ring there is always another, smaller ring. That longing, he warned, is the likeliest thing in the world to make a man who is not yet a scoundrel do the scoundrel's work: no one ever quite decides to become corrupt; he is simply drawn, hint by hint and drink by drink, across lines he never examined, for the pleasure of being drawn. That is precisely how the institute digests Mark — never by argument, always by inclusion.

And the bitter joke of Mark's story is this: he already possessed the innermost ring in existence, and had not noticed. Jane was the prize all along — the smallest, most exclusive society he could ever belong to, membership two, and he a founding member. He could not make himself care about it, because it was already his. But the universe cared. Lewis ends the book by having Venus herself — the celestial power of love in his cosmology — descend upon that little marriage, as though all heaven took one ordinary couple's reunion with total cosmic seriousness. The ring Mark spent the novel chasing was a corridor of doors with nothing at the end; the bond he despised was the thing the stars bent low to bless.

Against the ring, Lewis sets the household. St. Anne's is the non-ring: it admits a charwoman and a skeptic, not to make them feel chosen — there is no frisson of insiderhood at all — but to give them real work suited to what they really are. Real belonging through real contribution, real submission, real merit, under a real head. It is the one society in the book that works, and the only cure for ring-hunger anyone has ever found.

So where does the healing come from? If the head can fail by abdication and fail by usurpation; if niceness can be a mask and independence a starvation; if even the remedy of equality can curdle into a lie — where do we look to see headship whole? The church's answer has always been: look at Christ, long enough and closely enough that the suspicion of hierarchy falls off you. And no one in the English tongue has helped more people take that long look than Jonathan Edwards, in a sermon on the Lamb who is also the Lion.

From Edwards:

John was told of a Lion that had prevailed to open the book, and probably expected to see a lion in his vision; but while he is expecting, behold a Lamb appears to open the book, an exceeding diverse kind of creature from a lion. A lion is a devourer, one that is wont to make terrible slaughter of others; and no creature more easily falls a prey to him than a lamb.

He goes on:

There is an admirable conjunction of diverse excellencies in Jesus Christ. The lion and the lamb, though very diverse kinds of creatures, yet have each their peculiar excellencies. The lion excels in strength, and in the majesty of his appearance and voice: the lamb excels in meekness and patience. But we see that Christ is in the text compared to both, because the diverse excellencies of both wonderfully meet in him.

And hear him hold the two ends of the hierarchy together in one person:

There do meet in Jesus Christ infinite highness and infinite condescension. Christ, as he is God, is infinitely great and high above all. He is higher than the kings of the earth; for he is King of kings, and Lord of lords. So great is he, that all men, all kings and princes, are as worms of the dust before him; all nations are as the drop of the bucket, and the light dust of the balance.

He goes on:

And yet he is one of infinite condescension. None are so low or inferior, but Christ's condescension is sufficient to take a gracious notice of them. He condescends not only to take notice of princes and great men, but of those that are of meanest rank and degree, the poor of the world. Such as are commonly despised by their fellow creatures, Christ does not despise. Yea, so great is his condescension that it is enough to become their friend, to become their companion, to unite their souls to him in spiritual marriage; yea, to yield up himself to an ignominious death for them.

And then Edwards turns the blade on every one of us who has ever held a little authority:

Such a conjunction of infinite highness and low condescension, in the same person, is admirable. We see, by manifold instances, what a tendency a high station has in men, to make them of a quite contrary disposition. If one worm be a little exalted above another, by having more dust, or a bigger dunghill, how much does he make of himself! Christ condescends to wash our feet; but how would great men — or rather the bigger worms — account themselves debased by acts of far less condescension!

More dust, or a bigger dunghill. There is the institute in its cellar; there is the college in its committee room; there, if we are honest, is the scanning eye in our own heads. And against it all stands the one head who never abdicated and never usurped — whose very name in the novel is the clue, for what is a ransom? Hear the Lord himself, on the road to Jerusalem, when his own disciples were jockeying for the inner ring.

From the Gospel of Mark, in the King James Version:

But Jesus called them to him, and saith unto them, Ye know that they which are accounted to rule over the Gentiles exercise lordship over them; and their great ones exercise authority upon them. But so shall it not be among you: but whosoever will be great among you, shall be your minister: and whosoever of you will be the chiefest, shall be servant of all. For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.

The worthy head is the one who has shown himself willing to be under authority and to spend himself for the body — to become, himself, the ransom. And the apostle traces the whole arc, downward and then up, in the greatest hierarchy-hymn ever written.

From the epistle to the Philippians:

Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus: who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant, and was made in the likeness of men: and being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross.

He goes on:

Wherefore God also hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name: that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth; and that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.

Obedient unto death — wherefore highly exalted. Submission first, then the crown; the Lamb slain, therefore the Lion worthy. Every broken hierarchy in tonight's story is some refusal of that order: the usurper wants the exaltation without the obedience; the abdicator refuses both; and the household on the hill is simply a room where people have begun, haltingly, to practice it. The head who wears scars is the only head worth following — and, wonder of wonders, he is the one head that will never leave the body exposed.

We close with a hymn written for that head — the Lamb upon his throne. Crown Him with Many Crowns, by Matthew Bridges.

Crown him with many crowns, the Lamb upon his throne; hark how the heavenly anthem drowns all music but its own. Awake, my soul, and sing of him who died for thee, and hail him as thy matchless King through all eternity.

Crown him the Virgin's Son, the God incarnate born, whose arm those crimson trophies won which now his brow adorn; fruit of the mystic rose, as of that rose the stem; the root whence mercy ever flows, the Babe of Bethlehem.

Crown him the Lord of Love. Behold his hands and side, rich wounds, yet visible above, in beauty glorified. No angel in the sky can fully bear that sight, but downward bends his wondering eye at mysteries so bright.

Crown him the Lord of Life, who triumphed o'er the grave, and rose victorious in the strife for those he came to save. His glories now we sing, who died and rose on high, who died eternal life to bring, and lives that death may die.

Crown him the Lord of Heaven, enthroned in worlds above, crown him the King to whom is given the wondrous name of Love. Crown him with many crowns as thrones before him fall; crown him, ye kings, with many crowns, for he is King of all.

Good night.
