About this songA rebuke to the “touch not, taste not” frown patrol — matter isn't evil; God made the strawberry sweet and the wine at Cana. Grace isn't a diet.
Big ideaMatter is not evil and grace is not a diet: the God who "made the strawberry sweet" made the taste buds too, so the ascetic "touch not, taste not" creed is not extra-holy — it's a Gnostic counterfeit that adds rules to Christ's finished work.
Doctrine
Goodness of creation · anti-asceticism · common grace

Study this song

Teaching aids drawn from the song — for personal study or group discussion.

How the song moves

1
The frown patrol booked
Intro and Verse 1 put "Miss Prim" and her clipboard on trial for "competitive misery, flauntin' what they DON'T" — proud abstaining paraded as holiness — while the God "with the sun and the rain" quietly sweetens strawberries for everyone (Matthew 5:45).
2
Quoting the killjoy against himself
The Pre-Chorus lets the ascetics chant their own slogan, "Touch not! Taste not! Handle not the good!" — lifted almost verbatim from the very verse Paul cites in order to condemn it (Colossians 2:21).
3
Cana and the Preacher settle it
Verse 2 answers the sneer "a glutton and a wino" by pointing to the 150 gallons of good wine at Cana ("coulda made it grape juice, weak and thin") and to Ecclesiastes' command to eat and drink with a merry heart (John 2:1–11; Luke 7:34; Ecclesiastes 9:7).
4
Naming the counterfeit
The Bridge diagnoses the whole system: the touch-not creed has "an appearance of wise," "but it's Gnostic in a Sunday-mornin' disguise" — it can't kill a single sin, it only "puffs up the self," so "quit addin' rules to the finished, finished work" (Colossians 2:23; John 19:30).

Key terms & allusions

  • "Touch not, taste not, handle not" — The ascetic slogan of Colossians 2:21 — Paul quotes these man-made food-and-body prohibitions not to endorse them but to expose them as powerless against sin, which is exactly how the song deploys the line.
  • asceticism / the "frown patrol" — The impulse to earn holiness by self-denial and renouncing pleasures; 1 Timothy 4:3 names it directly as "forbidding to marry" and "commanding to abstain from foods" that God created to be received with thanksgiving.
  • Gnosticism — The ancient heresy (a "Sunday-mornin' disguise" here) that treats spirit as pure and physical matter as evil — the song's rebuttal, "matter ain't evil and the body ain't bad," is the Christian counter-claim that God called His material creation good.
  • the wedding at Cana — Jesus' first sign (John 2:1–11), where He turned roughly 120–180 gallons of water — the song's "hundred fifty gallons" — into fine wine, not "grape juice, weak and thin," making abundance and joy His opening miracle.
  • "a glutton and a wino" / "eat your bread… with a merry heart" — The first echoes the slur thrown at Jesus in Matthew 11:19 / Luke 7:34 ("a glutton and a drunkard, a friend of… sinners"); the second near-quotes Ecclesiastes 9:7, where "the Preacher" (Qoheleth) commands enjoyment of food and wine as God's approved gift.

Discussion questions

  1. Where does your church culture quietly reward "competitive misery" — treating what someone abstains from as proof of holiness — and how would 1 Timothy 4:4–5 reframe those unspoken scorecards?
  2. The bridge calls the touch-not creed "Gnostic in a Sunday-mornin' disguise." What present-day rules or attitudes in our community treat the body and physical pleasure as spiritually suspect, and why is that a counterfeit rather than extra devotion?
  3. Read Colossians 2:20–23 together: Paul says these rules have "an appearance of wisdom" but no power to restrain sin. Why is adding rules to "the finished work" so appealing to us, and what does genuine grace-formed self-control look like instead?

For contemplation

  1. Is there a good gift — food, drink, rest, beauty, laughter — that you deny yourself and secretly count as righteousness? What would it mean to receive it instead "with thanksgiving" (1 Timothy 4:4)?
  2. When you picture Jesus, is He nearer to the frowning clipboard or to the host who poured out 150 gallons of good wine at Cana? Where did that picture come from, and is it true?
  3. Ecclesiastes 9:7 tells you to eat your bread and drink your wine with a merry heart because God has already approved your work. Where are you still trying to earn what Christ has already finished?
