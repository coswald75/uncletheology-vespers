About this songThe risen Carpenter King who drew near — His risen wounds curing all doubt; He knows my name, my hairs, my tomorrows, and death itself is just an empty tomb.
Big ideaBecause the risen Christ — the despised carpenter of Nazareth — carried His wounds through death and out the other side, the believer's doubt is cured, his smallest hairs are counted, and the grave itself is emptied of its terror into a "godly happiness" the unbelieving world cannot account for.
Doctrine
The bodily resurrection of Christ and His victory over sin and death — Christus Victor, the risen wounds curing doubt and turning the tomb inside-out
Anchor text
John 20:24-29; Mark 6:3; Matthew 10:30; 1 Corinthians 15:54-57
Form
Reverb-drenched, major-key Laurel Canyon folk-rock — bright shoegaze in the Lord Huron vein, wide-open harmonies washing over a resurrection-morning glow rather than melancholy
Voices
Charles Wesley ("And Can It Be That I Should Gain," 1738), whose "Amazing love! how can it be" surfaces almost verbatim in the finale

Study this song

Teaching aids drawn from the song — for personal study or group discussion.

How the song moves

1
Verse 1 — doubt cured by the wounds
The song opens by looking outward at those who "live their lives without him" and cannot make it all make sense; the singer confesses he was a doubter too, until "His risen wounds" — the scars Thomas was invited to touch — did the curing. Faith here is not won by argument but by the marks of the crucified body still present in the risen one, and out of it "grows a godly happiness" (John 20:24-29).
2
Chorus — the Carpenter King draws near
The song names its subject: "That Nazarene drew near to me — Carpenter King." The hometown insult of Mark 6:3 ("Is not this the carpenter?") is turned into a title of glory. Incarnation flows straight into atonement — the working-man Messiah is also the King "by whose blood I am set free" (Mark 6:3).
3
Verse 2 — from cosmic to personal
The lens narrows from the crowd's sorrow and "the empty room" to the singer himself: "He knows my name, my hairs and my tomorrows." The providence is so fine-grained that death loses its weight and becomes "just an empty tomb" — grief reframed by resurrection (Matthew 10:30).
4
Finale — sin and death defeated, greeted as a friend
The song lands on triumph: "Both sin and death have been defeated," and the redeemed sinner is "as a friend now greeted." It closes borrowing Wesley's astonishment — "Amazing love — how can this be?" — the same wonder Paul voices when death is swallowed up and its sting is gone (1 Corinthians 15:54-57).

Key terms & allusions

  • That Nazarene / Carpenter King — Jesus of Nazareth, the despised backwater town (John 1:46, "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?"). "Carpenter" recalls Mark 6:3, where His own townsfolk sneer, "Is not this the carpenter?" The Greek is tektōn (τέκτων) — a craftsman who builds in wood or stone, a manual laborer. The song takes that dismissal and crowns it: the working-man's hands are the King's hands.
  • His risen wounds are curin all my doubt'n — The doubting-Thomas scene of John 20:24-29. Thomas refuses to believe unless he can put his finger in the nail-marks and his hand in Jesus' side; the risen Christ offers him exactly that, and Thomas answers, "My Lord and my God." The point the song presses: the resurrection body still bears its scars, and it is the wounds — not a wound-free glory — that heal unbelief.
  • He knows my name, my hairs and my tomorrows — Matthew 10:30, "But the very hairs of your head are all numbered." In context Jesus is telling the disciples not to fear those who can kill the body — the meticulous care of God (counting hairs) is the ground for fearlessness before death, which is exactly where the lyric turns next ("death itself is just an empty tomb").
  • death itself is just an empty tomb / sin and death defeated — 1 Corinthians 15:54-57: "Death is swallowed up in victory. O death, where is thy sting?" Paul stitches together Isaiah 25:8 and Hosea 13:14 to announce that the resurrection has drained death of its power. This is the "Christus Victor" note — Christ as conqueror — and the reason the tomb can be called merely "empty" rather than final.
  • Amazing love — how can this be? — A near-direct quotation of Charles Wesley's 1738 hymn "And Can It Be That I Should Gain": "Amazing love! how can it be / That Thou, my God, shouldst die for me?" Wesley wrote it just after his own conversion; the song borrows his stunned, questioning wonder as its closing posture — and pairs it with being "greeted as a friend," echoing Jesus' words in John 15:15, "I have called you friends."

Discussion questions

  1. The song keeps repeating "I don't know how they live their lives without him" — how they face sorrow, the empty room, the world unbroken. How should Christian wonder at unbelief express itself: as compassion, as evangelistic urgency, as gratitude, or all three? What does it become if that wonder curdles into condescension?
  2. Thomas's doubt is cured not by an argument but by the risen wounds. Where in your group's shared experience has doubt been answered more by encountering the crucified-and-risen Christ than by winning a debate — and what does that suggest about how we walk with doubters?
  3. The finale insists "both sin AND death have been defeated." Why does the song refuse to separate the two, and how does 1 Corinthians 15 tie forgiveness of sin and the conquest of the grave together into one victory?

For contemplation

  1. Matthew 10:30 says even your hairs are numbered. Sit with the specific "tomorrows" you are anxious about — where would it change your fear to believe He already knows your name and your next day?
  2. The song says death is "just an empty tomb." Is that true of how you actually feel about death right now? Name honestly where the sting still bites, and bring that exact place to the risen Christ.
  3. You are "as a friend now greeted." Do you relate to Jesus more as a distant King or as a near friend who has kissed your head? What would it look like this week to receive the friendship He offers rather than only serve the King?
