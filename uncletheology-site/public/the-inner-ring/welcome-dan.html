Study this song

Teaching aids drawn from the song — for personal study or group discussion.

How the song moves

1
'I see you, Dan. I read them all.'
The Director answers 'Somebody, anybody, see me' point for point: I saw what they missed, I caught what they didn't, I read them all. It is the counterfeit of Psalm 139 sung by a predator, the deepest true longing, to be fully read and still wanted, fulfilled by the wrong person for the worst end.
2
'Come stand where the deciding gets done'
This is the pure voice of the Inner Ring: there is a circle inside, the ones who move the world, and you have been on the wrong side of the glass. Lewis says almost no one is proof against the offer to be let in among the people who decide. It is offered here as intimacy, 'the pitch' lowered to a confidence.
3
The duet and the fatal yes
The two voices soar together, Dan buying in, and then the music drops out for one un-processed line, 'Where do I sign?' The narrator called it 'the fatal yes.' No argument was ever made; Dan is not persuaded, he is seen, and being seen is enough to make him sign.

Key terms & allusions

  • 'I see you' — The album's master key. Lewis's essay warns that the Ring conquers not by reason but by the sense of being singled out and let in. 'I see you' is the exploit: people will hand over anything to whoever says it, which is exactly what the closing tag reveals the machine has learned.
  • 'the wrong side of the glass' — The Ring's geography, an inside and an outside, with you always pressed against the outside looking in. Lewis: the terror of exclusion and the joy of admission are the levers; the Director names Dan's lifelong ache as being on the wrong side of the glass.
  • 'where the deciding gets done' — The seat of power the Ring dangles. It flatters Dan that his opinions belong at the center, priming the very 'baseless confidence' the Institute is actually harvesting; he thinks he is being invited to decide, when he is the thing being decided upon.
  • 'Where do I sign?' (the fatal yes) — Dan's consent, given 'quiet, no processing.' Lewis's point exactly: the descent into the Ring is not a dramatic bargain but a soft, reasonable yes to a warm man, the way, the narrator says, 'it is nearly always done.'

Discussion questions

  1. The Director never argues; he just makes Dan feel seen. Why is being seen more persuasive than any case he could have made?
  2. 'You've been watching from the wrong side of the glass.' How does the Ring turn a real experience of exclusion into leverage?
  3. Dan says 'Where do I sign?' before he knows any terms. What does it reveal about us that we will consent to almost anything once we feel truly noticed?

For contemplation

  1. What would it take for someone to get me to say 'Where do I sign?', and does that price tell me where my heart actually lives?
  2. Christ already reads every word I never showed anyone and calls me his own. Why do I keep going to counterfeits to hear 'I see you'?
