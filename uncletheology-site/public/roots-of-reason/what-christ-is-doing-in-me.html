Study this song

Teaching aids drawn from the song — for personal study or group discussion.

How the song moves

1
The verdict isn’t theirs
They meant it for evil, but it just dug a well; Heaven turns the whole curse into a verse (Genesis 50:20; 1 Peter 1:6–7).
2
Peter’s own story
The sword in the garden, the threefold denial by the fire, then restoration on the shore — “feed My sheep” (John 18:10; 21:15–17).
3
Living hope, living stones
Born again to an imperishable inheritance; once nobody, now a royal priesthood, a holy nation built on the rejected cornerstone (1 Peter 1:3–4; 2:9).
4
Suffer like Christ
He was reviled but didn’t revile; so arm with the same mind, bless the blow, and entrust your soul to the faithful Creator (1 Peter 2:21–23; 4:19).

Key terms & allusions

  • “what Christ is doing through me trumps what they’re doing to me” — The hook — God’s sovereignty over a believer’s suffering (Genesis 50:20; Romans 8:28).
  • living stones / royal priesthood — Peter’s image of the church built on Christ the cornerstone (1 Peter 2:5–9; Psalm 118:22).
  • “He left the stencil” — Echoes the hypogramos of track 1: Christ as the pattern to trace in suffering (1 Peter 2:21).
  • “took an ear off a man in the garden” — Peter cutting off Malchus’s ear — the hothead who later writes “entrust it to the Lord” (John 18:10).

Discussion questions

  1. “What Christ is doing through me trumps what they’re doing to me.” How might that reframe a community facing opposition?
  2. Peter went from drawing a sword to counseling people to entrust themselves to God. How does his failure-and-restoration shape his counsel on suffering?
  3. The song says to “bless the blow.” What does blessing those who wrong us look like together as a church?

For contemplation

  1. Where do you need the reframe — “what Christ is doing through me trumps what they’re doing to me” — right now?
  2. What would it look like to “bless the blow” with someone who has wronged you?
  3. Can you “entrust the whole soul to the One who made you” today?
